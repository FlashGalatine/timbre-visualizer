# Drive Timbre with a local heartbeat

> Research for [issue #2](https://github.com/FlashGalatine/timbre-visualizer/issues/2), written 14 November 2026. It rests on public documentation and on Timbre's own code. Nothing here was tested on a heart-rate device or on a running Streamer.bot; the checks that need doing by hand are in the table at the end. Nothing is built yet.

**Yes: the streamer's live heart rate can drive Timbre in place of sound, and it is worth building, but only along a local route.** One kind of source meets the owner's ruling that heart-rate data "should never be stored remotely" by design: a standard Bluetooth heart-rate sensor. That means a chest strap such as the Polar H10, or a watch with a native broadcast mode, such as the Pixel Watch 2 and later or the Fitbit Charge 6. A small helper on the streaming PC reads the sensor and passes each reading to Streamer.bot, which forwards it to the overlay pages over the connection they already hold. The two cloud relays that Streamer.bot supports out of the box do not clear that bar on their own. **Pulsoid's terms say it saves BPM readings and timestamps, with no stated retention period, so it fails.** HypeRate says it keeps no heart-rate time series, but with the user's consent it stores per-session maximum, minimum and average BPM for up to 60 days, so it passes only with that consent withheld, and only on the strength of a policy no one has audited. Fitbit's cloud API, the first source the owner named, cannot drive animation at all: readings arrive only after the device syncs, and the legacy API was due to be switched off in late October or early November 2026. Every live route delivers a whole-number BPM about once a second, not individual beats. The overlay should therefore synthesise its own beat and write it into the four values every effect already reads. That gives all 51 effects a heartbeat mode without editing any effect file, plus a separate pulse value for the handful of effects, such as Valentine's Hearts and Pulse Rings, that already have a natural pulse. Showing the BPM number stays the streamer's choice, as the owner ruled. But hiding the number does not hide the rate if the animation beats at it, so the panel also needs a mode where the tempo stays fixed. Before any code is written, hands-on checks must confirm three things: that the owner's device delivers readings to a Windows 11 PC, that Streamer.bot can relay a reading every second without falling behind or logging the values, and that the pulse stays under the photosensitivity limit of three flashes per second.

## 1. Only straps and broadcasting watches are both live and private

The issue asks first where a heart rate can come from ([issue #2](https://github.com/FlashGalatine/timbre-visualizer/issues/2)). The owner's original request names sources "like Fitbits, smart watches, or any device that could provide heartbeat API data" (project owner, 14 November 2026). The research splits those cleanly in two. Fitness platforms collect heart rate into their own clouds and serve it later through web APIs. Broadcasting devices send the standard Bluetooth Heart Rate service (0x180D) to any nearby receiver as each reading is taken. **Only the second kind is fast enough to animate, and only the second adds nothing to anyone's servers.**

### Fitbit's cloud runs minutes late, but its newer devices broadcast live

**Fitbit's cloud cannot drive animation.** Heart rate reaches a Fitbit API only after the tracker syncs to the phone and then to the cloud. Fitbit forum moderators put that sync at intervals of about fifteen minutes ([Fitbit community](https://community.fitbit.com/t5/Web-API-Development/Data-Synchronization-Timing-for-Heart-Rate-Intraday-Data-via-Fitbit-Web-API/m-p/5733233)); that comes from forum posts read at search-snippet level, and no measured figure was found.

Google's own sources disagree on when the legacy Fitbit Web API switches off. The Google Health API page gives 2 November 2026, and Fitbit's developer blog gives 30 October 2026 ([Google Health API](https://developers.google.com/health/about), [Fitbit developer blog](https://dev.fitbit.com/blog/2025-09-26-important-updates-to-the-fitbit-web-apis/)). Both dates have passed, but the researcher did not test whether the endpoint is actually off. The replacement, the Google Health API, offers intraday data and webhooks but describes no real-time streaming. Every one of its permission scopes is classed as Restricted, which requires a privacy and security review ([Google Health API](https://developers.google.com/health/about)).

**The live Fitbit routes run over Bluetooth instead.** Google's support page says the **Fitbit Charge 6, Fitbit Edge, Fitbit Air and Pixel Watch 2 or newer share heart rate through the standard Bluetooth Heart Rate Profile** ([Google support](https://support.google.com/googlehealth/answer/14236705?hl=en)). The Charge 6 can feed one receiver at a time, the Pixel Watch 3 and later up to two, and broadcasting costs battery.

Older Fitbit OS watches (Versa, Sense, Ionic) can reach a stream only through Pulsoid's watch app ([Pulsoid FAQ](https://pulsoid.net/faq)). Fitbit's app gallery stops accepting third-party apps for those watches on 28 November 2026; after that, a deleted or factory-reset app cannot be reinstalled ([The 5K Runner](https://the5krunner.com/2026/11/09/quick-read-fitbit-shuts-third-party-apps-out-of-its-gallery/)). The researcher infers that new installs of Pulsoid's app become impossible after that date, but did not verify it.

### Other watches broadcast too, with Apple and Samsung the exceptions

Garmin watches have a Broadcast Heart Rate mode. It was historically ANT+ only, with Bluetooth added from the Fenix 6 beta in 2020 onward ([Garmin manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/forerunner945/EN-GB/GUID-D8D363C2-0690-48D4-95E2-A3557E7D53C2.html), [DC Rainmaker](https://dcrainmaker.com/2020/04/garmin-wearable-broadcasting.html)). A third-party Myzone document lists the Forerunner 245 to 970 and the Fenix 6 to 8 Pro as able to broadcast over Bluetooth, but it may be out of date ([Myzone](https://www.myzone.org/hubfs/Garmin%20Integration/myzone-Garmin-Device-Compatability-Overview.pdf)). One Forerunner 970 owner reported Bluetooth broadcast failing while ANT+ worked ([Garmin forum](https://forums.garmin.com/sports-fitness/running-multisport/f/forerunner-970/416682/bluetooth-on-broadcast-hr-not-working)). Garmin's newly reopened personal API key only pulls stored data and is not real-time ([The 5K Runner](https://the5krunner.com/2026/10/22/garmin-personal-api/)).

Several other wearables also present themselves as standard Bluetooth sensors. Amazfit's Heart Rate Push needs Zepp OS 3.0 or later and, on some watches, works only during a workout ([HypeRate on Amazfit](https://blog.hyperate.io/post/amazfit-heart-rate-push-live-bluetooth-heart-rate-streaming-with-zepp-os-hyperate-compatible/)). Whoop's HR Broadcast does so according to community and unofficial sources only ([Whoop community](https://www.community.whoop.com/t/broadcast-heartrate-hr-not-working-with-wahoo-elemnt/6590)). The list also includes the Xiaomi Smart Band 10's Share HR ([xiaomiforall](https://xiaomiforall.com/xiaomi-smart-band-10-cycling-heart-rate-tracking/)) and the Polar Verity Sense ([Verity Sense specs](https://the5krunner.com/specs/polar/verity-sense/)). Oura has no live route; a third-party guide puts its API data roughly a day late ([Open Wearables](https://www.openwearables.io/blog/oura-api-accessing-ring-data-sleep-hrv-readiness)). Huawei, Coros and Suunto were not established either way.

**Apple Watch is the awkward case.** Coverage of watchOS 26 shows no native heart-rate broadcast ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/stories/watchos-26-the-macstories-review/3/)). No Apple primary source was found to confirm or deny one, so the idea that recent watchOS versions added it remains unconfirmed. Third-party apps such as HeartCast and HeartBLE make the watch act as a Bluetooth sensor during a workout ([HeartCast](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1499771124), [HeartBLE](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6758879454)). Their listings leave unclear whether the signal leaves the watch directly or goes through the iPhone.

Samsung's Galaxy Watch also lacks a native broadcast ([Samsung community](https://us.community.samsung.com/t5/Galaxy-Watch/Feature-Request-Heart-Rate-Broadcasting-via-BLE-for-Galaxy-Watch/td-p/3314341)). The Heart for Bluetooth app fills the gap on Wear OS 3.5 and later, though users report that it reconnects unreliably ([Heart for Bluetooth](https://sites.google.com/view/heartforbluetooth/troubleshooting)). Older Galaxy watches that run Tizen rather than Wear OS have a different local option. HeartRateToWeb has the watch push readings to a local IP address and port, and an OBS browser source reads them from 127.0.0.1. One third-party document suggests a delay of at least two seconds ([OBS forum](https://obsproject.com/forum/resources/heartratetoweb.1038), [vrc-osc-tizenos-hrm](https://www.github.com/N0rule/vrc-osc-tizenos-hrm)). Those details come from search summaries only, and the researchers disagree: the watch-platforms note found nothing on HeartRateToWeb, while the relay-services note found it. It stays an open item.

### A chest strap tracks best at the moments a stream cares about

**A chest strap is the most accurate source, and the least tangled up with other services.** A 2025 systematic review found the Polar H10 the most evaluated chest sensor, and in strong agreement with an ECG at rest and in light to moderate activity ([Sensors 2025 review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12526813/)). A 2025 comparison of wrist and arm optical sensors against the H10 found a mean absolute error of 6.41 bpm for one device, with reliability ranging from 3.64% to 23.03% depending on the activity ([JMIR Cardio](https://cardio.jmir.org/2025/1/e67110/PDF)).

Wrist devices also get worse at exactly the moments a stream cares about. A 2025 study found all of them less accurate at the start of movement and at large sudden changes in heart rate ([Hasselt study](https://documentserver.uhasselt.be/bitstream/1942/47708/1/Accuracy%20of%20Heart%20Rate%20Measurement%20Under%20Transient%20States_%20A%20Validation%20Study%20of%20Wearables%20for%20Real-Life%20Monitoring.pdf)). An earlier study found an Apple Watch error of 2.4% to 5.1%, against 3.9% to 13.5% for a Fitbit Charge HR2, as exercise intensity rose ([PubMed 30657025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30657025/)).

HypeRate Desktop's offline Bluetooth Direct mode shows that a strap needs no phone, app, account or internet connection to stream ([HypeRate Desktop](https://blog.hyperate.io/post/the-best-free-heart-rate-overlay-for-streamers-and-gamers-hyperate-desktop/)). The researcher on local routes found no citable source on how much optical watches lag or smooth their readings, so the common view that they trail by several seconds remains unmeasured.

### Relay services cover every device, but only through their clouds

Pulsoid and HypeRate work with all these devices by forwarding readings from watch apps to their own clouds. Between them they cover Apple Watch, Wear OS, some Fitbits, Garmin by broadcast, Polar and generic straps ([Pulsoid FAQ](https://pulsoid.net/faq), [HypeRate supported devices](https://hyperate.io/supported-devices)). Stromno and Heartwear do the same through their own back ends: a website dashboard for Stromno, and Firebase for Heartwear ([Stromno](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/stromno/id1538966480), [Heartwear](https://github.com/domi2803/heartwear)). Their storage practices were not checked, so neither can be cleared against the ruling.

HypeRate also sells three products beyond its relay. These are its Desktop app in cloud mode, which uses a session ID, its OBS plugin, and a Web Bluetooth page at webbluetooth.hyperate.io ([HypeRate Desktop](https://blog.hyperate.io/post/the-best-free-heart-rate-overlay-for-streamers-and-gamers-hyperate-desktop/), [HypeRate OBS plugin](https://obs.hyperate.io/about.html), [HypeRate Web Bluetooth](https://webbluetooth.hyperate.io/)). The researchers presume all three route readings through HypeRate's servers for the stream ID, but did not verify it. They therefore carry the same conditional status as the HypeRate relay. The Web Bluetooth page is easy to confuse with the local Chrome-tab reader discussed in section 2, but the two are not the same. HypeRate's page presumably uploads; a page served from localhost does not. Only Desktop's Bluetooth Direct mode is described as working fully offline. Pulsoid and HypeRate are judged in full in section 2.

The table rates each source against the owner's two tests: whether it is live enough to animate, and whether it stores data remotely.

**One question the research cannot settle belongs to the owner.** A Pixel Watch or Charge 6 broadcasting over Bluetooth gives Timbre's route no remote copy. But the same watch normally keeps syncing its heart rate to Google's cloud, as Garmin, Whoop, Zepp and Apple Health do for their own devices. The researchers treated that vendor-side storage as common knowledge and did not verify it vendor by vendor. If the ruling means that Timbre must add no remote copy, every broadcasting device passes. If it means that no remote copy of the stream's heartbeat may exist anywhere, only a strap worn without its companion app passes, and the setup guide must say so.

## 2. A local helper feeding Streamer.bot keeps every reading on the PC

The issue offers three routes to the overlay page: A, through Streamer.bot; B, straight from a service to the page; and C, a small helper program on the PC. **The research supports C feeding into A, and rejects B.** It allows Streamer.bot's own integrations only with their storage caveats spelled out.

### Route A: Streamer.bot's built-in integrations are easy, but they bring the cloud with them

**Route A works today without any code in Streamer.bot.** Streamer.bot has native Pulsoid and HypeRate integrations. Pulsoid connects through an OAuth login, HypeRate needs only the user's HypeRate ID, and both can auto-connect and auto-reconnect ([Streamer.bot Pulsoid guide](https://docs.streamer.bot/guide/integrations/pulsoid), [Streamer.bot HypeRate guide](https://docs.streamer.bot/guide/integrations/hyperate)).

Streamer.bot's WebSocket server exposes the readings as events, which a client receives after sending a `Subscribe` request. `Pulsoid.HeartRatePulse` carries `heartRate` and `measuredAt`, and `HypeRate.HeartRatePulse` carries `heartRate` alone ([events index](https://docs.streamer.bot/api/websocket/events), [Pulsoid event](https://docs.streamer.bot/api/websocket/events/pulsoid/heart-rate-pulse), [HypeRate event](https://docs.streamer.bot/api/websocket/events/hype-rate/heart-rate-pulse), [WebSocket requests](https://docs.streamer.bot/api/websocket/requests)). Timbre's overlay could subscribe to these on the connection it already opens, with no action, no C# and no Pulsoid or HypeRate credential in the page.

Three details need care. First, the docs show event categories capitalised in examples but lowercase in the output of `GetEvents`, and do not say which form `Subscribe` requires. Timbre's own `shared/panel-client-sb.js` notes that its `general` key must be lowercase, which suggests the same applies here, but that is an inference to check on a 1.0.4 install. Second, since version 0.2.3 both integrations send a pulse only when the value changes ([v0.2.3 changelog](https://streamer.bot/changelogs/v0.2.3)). A steady heart rate therefore produces no events at all, which section 6 returns to. Third, the HypeRate integration was broken until 1.0.3, and 1.0.7 fixed a crash on connecting ([v1.0.3](https://streamer.bot/changelogs/v1.0.3), [v1.0.7](https://streamer.bot/changelogs/v1.0.7)). **If Timbre offers HypeRate, the minimum is Streamer.bot 1.0.7**, not the 1.0.4 that Timbre is tested on. The version labels on individual docs pages contradict the changelogs and should not be trusted.

**The catch is that these integrations are cloud relays, and Pulsoid keeps the readings.** Pulsoid's terms, read through a summarising fetch, say that heart-rate data is saved securely and not directly linked to personal data. They list beats per minute and timestamp among what its mobile app collects, and give no retention period ([Pulsoid legal](https://pulsoid.net/legal)). Its API serves statistics over 24 hours, 7 days and 30 days, and its paid BRO plan exports heart-rate history ([Pulsoid statistics](https://docs.pulsoid.net/heart-rate-and-sensor-data/read-statistics.md), [Pulsoid FAQ](https://pulsoid.net/faq)). The researcher infers from the 30-day window that readings are kept at least that long, though Pulsoid states no figure. Its WebSocket API has an `ingestion_pause` message, but nothing fetched says that pausing stops storage ([Pulsoid WebSocket v2](https://docs.pulsoid.net/heart-rate-and-sensor-data/read-data-via-websocket-v2.md)). **Pulsoid fails the ruling.** It would pass only if Pulsoid confirmed a no-storage mode, and none of the fetched pages describes one.

**HypeRate passes only on conditions.** Its data-privacy page was also read through a summarising fetch, so its wording needs a word-for-word check ([HypeRate data privacy](https://hyperate.io/data-privacy.html)). It says HypeRate stores no heart-rate time series and processes heart rate live only. But with the user's consent, it stores per-session aggregates: maximum, minimum and average BPM, duration and game. These are kept for up to 60 days on a rolling basis, and can be deleted from the app or by email. Those aggregates are heart-rate data held remotely. HypeRate therefore fits the ruling only when the streamer withholds that consent, and only on a policy no one has audited. **Whether Streamer.bot's HypeRate connection opens a session that would generate such aggregates is unknown.**

Streamer.bot itself is local software. But its docs say nothing about whether pulse values end up in action history, logs or persisted global variables, or what the integrations send to the services beyond what they need to work ([Variables guide](https://docs.streamer.bot/guide/variables), [Pulsoid guide](https://docs.streamer.bot/guide/integrations/pulsoid)). That has to be inspected on a real install.

### Route B: going straight from a service to the page is the weakest option

**Route B combines the cloud's storage problem with credentials in the page.** Every service-to-page connection is still a cloud relay, so it inherits Pulsoid's storage and HypeRate's conditions, and each service also wants a credential held in the page.

Pulsoid's WebSocket (`wss://dev.pulsoid.net/api/v2/data/real_time`) needs a token. A manually issued token requires the paid BRO plan or its trial ([Pulsoid manual token](https://docs.pulsoid.net/getting-started/access-token-management/manual-token-issuing.md)). That plan costs $4.99 a month or $45 a year according to a search summary of a jumbled pricing page, which needs a live check ([Pulsoid pricing, via search summary](https://lamal.pulsoid.net/pricing)). The OAuth device flow needs a registered client ID, and says nothing about open-source clients ([Pulsoid device flow](https://docs.pulsoid.net/getting-started/access-token-management/oauth-2-device-authorization-flow.md)).

HypeRate's free "Research & Indie" API key comes through a request form ([HypeRate API](https://hyperate.io/api)), and its reference repository says keys must never be committed ([HypeRate-Websocket-API](https://github.com/HypeRate/HypeRate-Websocket-API)). So every streamer would need a key of their own. HypeRate's endpoint is also unsettled. The official repository gives `wss://app.hyperate.io/ws/:deviceId` with a ping every 15 seconds ([WEBSOCKET_API.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HypeRate/HypeRate-Websocket-API/main/docs/WEBSOCKET_API.md)). A search summary of its DevDocs repository gives `/socket/websocket` with a 10-second keep-alive, but that repository returned 404 when fetched directly ([DevDocs, via search summary](https://github.com/HypeRate/DevDocs)). Community threads report keys refused with 401 and 403 errors ([HypeRate help](https://help.hyperate.io/support/api/api-key-stopped-working-3400)).

There is a structural problem too, an inference from Timbre's design. Settings travel from the control panel through the "Timbre Push" action to every overlay page, so a token kept in the config would be broadcast to all of them.

### Route C: a local reader is the only route that is private by construction

**A local reader on the PC is the only route that keeps every reading on the machine by design.** Windows reads such a sensor by finding the device, subscribing to the Heart Rate Measurement characteristic (0x2A37), and receiving each reading through a `ValueChanged` event ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/uwp/devices-sensors/gatt-client)). Each reading holds a flags byte, the BPM as an 8- or 16-bit value, optional energy expended, and optional beat-to-beat (RR) intervals in units of 1/1024 of a second ([Adafruit driver](https://github.com/adafruit/Adafruit_CircuitPython_BLE_Heart_Rate/blob/master/adafruit_ble_heart_rate.py)). That layout comes from third-party driver code; the Bluetooth SIG specification itself was not fetched.

**Reading Bluetooth inside OBS is a dead end.** An OBS forum report from September 2025 says the Web Bluetooth device picker never appears inside a browser source, even through Interact, and nobody replied with a fix ([OBS forum](https://obsproject.com/forum/threads/bluetooth-connection-in-browser-source.190713/)). That is a single report, but no documented switch to enable it was found. A normal Chrome or Edge tab is a possible alternative. Chrome supports Web Bluetooth on Windows from a secure page, such as one served from localhost ([Chrome docs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/capabilities/bluetooth)). One MIT-licensed project already uses such a tab to read a strap and relay it to an OBS page ([zfox23](https://github.com/zfox23/bluetooth-heartrate-over-http)). The cost is a tab that must stay open, and a device picker the user must click.

Ready-made readers exist, but none fits exactly.

| Reader | What it offers | What is unknown or missing |
|---|---|---|
| HRWS ([OBS forum resource](https://obsproject.com/forum/resources/hrws.2194/)) | Free Windows tool that turns any Bluetooth heart-rate device into a WebSocket feed for OBS; says everything runs locally; last updated April 2026 | Licence, source availability and message format |
| religa's pulse-overlay ([GitHub](https://github.com/religa/pulse-overlay)) | Python server on `ws://localhost:8765` that includes RR intervals as `rr_ms` | MIT licence known only from a search summary |
| jlennox's HeartRate ([GitHub](https://github.com/jlennox/HeartRate)) | MIT-licensed C# tray program with UDP and file output | No WebSocket; last changed May 2022 |
| HypeRate Desktop ([HypeRate blog](https://blog.hyperate.io/post/the-best-free-heart-rate-overlay-for-streamers-and-gamers-hyperate-desktop/)) | Fully offline Bluetooth Direct mode | Draws its own always-on-top window instead of feeding a browser source |
| pulse-layer ([ecosyste.ms listing](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fwielorzeczownik%2Fpulse-layer)) | MIT-licensed Rust reader for a smart ring that claims to run with no cloud | Message format, maintenance and Windows support not checked |

Two other options fit poorly. Reading Bluetooth inside a Streamer.bot C# action is unproven. The docs never state the .NET runtime, their VS Code recipe targets .NET Framework 4.8.1, and Windows' Bluetooth types need extra setup on that framework ([Streamer.bot VS Code recipe](https://docs.streamer.bot/api/csharp/recipes/visual-studio-code), [apisof.net](https://apisof.net/catalog/722360de19352e3b74694c9da143d6d8)). ANT+, which older Garmins use, needs a USB stick, a driver swap to WinUSB with the Zadig tool, and Garmin Express closed ([ant-plus](https://unpkg.com/ant-plus@0.2.0/README.md)).

### The recommended shape: the helper feeds Streamer.bot, which feeds the overlays

**The recommended route is C feeding into A.** A small helper on the PC reads the strap or watch. It sends each reading to Streamer.bot's WebSocket with the documented `DoAction` request, passing the BPM, and the RR intervals when present, as arguments ([WebSocket requests](https://docs.streamer.bot/api/websocket/requests)). A short action rebroadcasts them with `CPH.WebsocketBroadcastJson`, which reaches every connected client ([WebsocketBroadcastJson](https://docs.streamer.bot/api/csharp/methods/core/websocket/websocket-broadcast-json)). The overlays then receive the readings the same way they already receive settings.

This keeps Timbre's design of one Streamer.bot connection per page. It also lets one overlay handler serve both the local helper and, if the owner allows it, Streamer.bot's HypeRate events. And it leaves Timbre holding no account credential.

It costs one action run per reading. For events about once a second, Streamer.bot advises a blocking queue and a fast action, but it publishes no throughput figures ([Pulsoid trigger](https://docs.streamer.bot/api/triggers/integrations/pulsoid/heart-rate-pulse)). It is also unknown whether action history records the arguments. **If hands-on testing shows lag, backlog or logging, the fallback is a direct local connection.** The helper would serve a localhost WebSocket that the overlay reads directly, which is how pulse-overlay already works.

## 3. A synthesised beat, written into the values effects already read, moves every effect

### Relays send a number, so the overlay must make the beat

**No live relay delivers heartbeats; they deliver a number.** Pulsoid's WebSocket message carries `heartRate.bpm` as a whole number and documents no RR intervals ([Pulsoid WebSocket v2](https://docs.pulsoid.net/heart-rate-and-sensor-data/read-data-via-websocket-v2.md)). HypeRate's carries `payload.hr`, from a repository that describes itself as heart rate only ([WEBSOCKET_API.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HypeRate/HypeRate-Websocket-API/main/docs/WEBSOCKET_API.md)). Streamer.bot passes on a whole number, and only when it changes.

How often the number arrives depends on the device and the service. Pulsoid's docs say most monitors report once a second ([Pulsoid HTTP](https://docs.pulsoid.net/heart-rate-and-sensor-data/read-latest-heart-rate-via-http.md)). A search summary of Pulsoid's blog puts the Apple Watch at about one reading every five seconds ([Pulsoid Apple Watch post](https://blog.pulsoid.net/post/pulsoid-supports-apple-watch-natively)). HypeRate's claim of under 200 ms end to end comes from a search summary of its blog feed, with no measurement behind it ([HypeRate blog feed](https://blog.hyperate.io/feed.xml)). Real beat timing exists only in a strap's RR intervals over a direct Bluetooth link. Even those, the researchers infer, arrive batched with each reading and describe beats that have already passed.

**The overlay should therefore make its own beat, as prior art does.** HypeRate's desktop overlay says its animation speed follows the reported BPM automatically ([HypeRate Desktop](https://blog.hyperate.io/post/the-best-free-heart-rate-overlay-for-streamers-and-gamers-hyperate-desktop/)). VRChat bridges send only a whole-number BPM ([HypeRate OSC guide](https://blog.hyperate.io/post/how-to-stream-your-real-heart-rate-into-vrchat-hyperate-osc-hr-bridge-complete-guide/)). No tool examined publishes its smoothing method, so Timbre has no convention to match.

The method that follows is the researchers' engineering inference, not a sourced recipe. Keep a beat phase that advances every frame by `dt × bpm / 60`, and fire a beat each time it wraps. Smooth the BPM with a one-pole filter or a slew limit. Never reset the phase when a new reading arrives, so the tempo bends instead of stuttering. A lub-dub can be two decaying pulses about 0.25 to 0.35 of a beat apart.

For an ECG-like trace, adding up five Gaussian bumps at the P, Q, R, S and T angles of the ECGSYN model (−70, −15, 0, 15 and 100 degrees) gives the familiar shape without solving the model's equations. ECGSYN itself is GPL v3, so Timbre can reuse the published idea but must not copy its code ([PhysioNet ECGSYN](https://physionet.org/content/ecgsyn/1.0.0/)). When a strap supplies RR intervals, the same phase can be nudged toward the measured interval. Whether viewers can tell a pulse locked to real beats from a synthesised one is unstudied. Heart-rate variability, which Nevermind's designers used to infer a player's stress ([Affectiva](https://blog.affectiva.com/finding-the-essence-of-fear-in-nevermind)), needs those RR intervals, so the researchers place it out of scope for a first version.

### Filling the existing values reaches all 51 effects; a new value reaches only the edited ones

**Writing the beat into the four existing values reaches every effect at once.** Today `overlay/audio-capture.js` fills four values every 33 ms from the microphone. They are declared in `overlay/core.js`, and the 51 effect files in `overlay/factories/` read them as the table shows (Timbre `main`, read first-hand).

| Value | What it holds | Effect files that read it |
|---|---|---|
| `frequencies` | 32 frequency bands | 48 |
| `waveform` | 64 samples | 24 |
| `micPeak` | Loudest instant, 0 to 1 | 6 |
| `micActive` | Whether the mic is live | 50 |

The one remaining effect, Thanksgiving Harvest, gets its input through `overlay/scene-kit.js`. A heartbeat source that fills the same four values therefore drives every effect with no effect file changed, including the shared afterglow, helix and scene-kit modules. A new value, by contrast, reaches only the effects edited to read it.

So the answer to whether to fill the existing values or add a new one is both. Fill the four so that heartbeat mode works everywhere on day one, and publish a separate pulse value for the effects tuned to it: smoothed BPM, beat phase, a beat flag and a 0-to-1 intensity. A plausible fill, again an inference, puts the beat envelope in `micPeak`, a decaying thump in the low bands of `frequencies`, and the ECG-like trace in `waveform`. It also holds `micActive` true while readings are live, so effects stay out of their idle animation.

### How fast is fast: map BPM onto the streamer's own range

**"Fast" has to be relative to the streamer, not absolute.** The mapping the research supports is heart-rate reserve: intensity = (bpm − rest) ÷ (max − rest), clamped to 0..1, with rest and max set by the streamer.

If the streamer wants an estimated maximum, Tanaka's meta-analysis of 18,712 subjects gives 208 − 0.7 × age. That is more accurate than 220 − age in older adults, but carries a standard error of about 10 bpm ([Tanaka 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11153730/)). Asking for an age is itself collecting personal data, so it should stay optional.

For a seated streamer, the useful range sits far below maximum. A commercial horror-game panel of about 200 players, not peer reviewed, recorded resting baselines near 65 bpm, game averages of 94 to 99, and spikes of 131 to 135 ([Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/posts/scariest-horror-video-games)). That suggests starting defaults of about 65 and 140; this is an inference from one panel, not a published norm.

**Smoothing must not erase the spikes that make the feature worth having.** Startle studies, read through search summaries, find heart-rate acceleration peaking about 3 to 4 seconds after a sudden noise and holding for 4 to 6 seconds ([Turpin](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/9037/1/Turpin_Habituation.pdf)). A smoothing time constant of a second or two keeps a scare visible. A running baseline over the session is a reasonable fallback when the streamer sets nothing, but no source backs a particular window.

### Instead of sound or beside it: offer both

**Heartbeat should sit beside sound as well as replace it.** The research tested neither arrangement, so this is a design recommendation. Offer three modes. Sound is today's behaviour. Heartbeat replaces the microphone's values with the synthesised ones. Both lets the heart set tempo and overall energy while the microphone keeps the spectral detail most effects were built for. One simple blend for Both scales the mic's `frequencies` by the heart intensity and takes the larger of the two `micPeak` values. Both mode matters because 48 effects read the 32-band spectrum, and a synthetic thump has little to put in most of those bands.

## 4. The effects that already pulse lead, and the spectrum-heavy ones need an eye test

**A short list of effects already has a natural heartbeat reading** in the codebase (Timbre `main`, `overlay/factories/`). They are Valentine's Hearts, with its `pulseStrength` setting; Pulse Rings; Orbit, whose sun swells with the low end; the hearts shed by the Pride flag effects; and the characters Blob, Owl and Television. Television's mouth is drawn from the live waveform, so it would naturally become an ECG trace. These effects should read the new pulse value directly and be tuned by eye.

The six effects that read `micPeak` were built to react to a single peak, so they get a clean thump from the beat envelope with no change: Black Hole, DNA, Comet, Fireflies, Orbit and Pulse Rings.

Everything else works through the filled values but was designed for music. Effects that lean on fine detail across the 32 bands will receive a repetitive low-band pulse that may look thin. No source can settle that, so the owner should judge it in OBS (inference). **The practical rule is to ship heartbeat mode for every effect and tune that short list.** The owner can then mark any effect where heartbeat alone looks poor, so that Both becomes its suggested mode.

## 5. Settings live in the existing panel, and readings never enter the config

**Heartbeat settings fit Timbre's existing settings path, but the readings must stay off it.** Today the control panel sends the config to a Streamer.bot C# action named "Timbre Push", which broadcasts it to every overlay page; no audio crosses any wire (Timbre `main`, `shared/panel-client-sb.js`).

Heartbeat readings must never be written into the pushed config, saved panel settings, Streamer.bot global variables, or any file. The config goes to every page, and persisted globals are stored by definition, in a place and at a frequency the docs do not describe ([Variables guide](https://docs.streamer.bot/guide/variables)). Credentials stay out of Timbre too. With the helper or Streamer.bot's own integrations, the only credential the page holds is Streamer.bot's WebSocket authentication, if that is enabled.

Naming needs care. The Pride flags already have a setting named `heartRate` in `shared/viz-config.js` that sets how often hearts appear, and Streamer.bot's events also use `heartRate` for BPM. The new settings therefore need a distinct prefix, such as `hb`.

The table below gives the proposed settings. The defaults are this report's recommendations, and none is sourced beyond the reason given.

| Setting | Proposed default | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Input: Sound, Heartbeat or Both | Sound | Existing behaviour stays unchanged until the streamer opts in |
| Heart source: local sensor, or Streamer.bot's HypeRate integration if the owner allows it | Local sensor | The only source clean by construction; Pulsoid not offered |
| Show BPM number | Off | The owner leaves display to the streamer; off keeps it a deliberate choice |
| Pulse tempo follows heart rate | On, with a tempo-free option | A pulse viewers can count reveals the rate even with the number hidden |
| Resting and maximum BPM | 65 and 140 | Heart-rate reserve mapping; starting values from the horror-game panel |
| Smoothing | About 1 to 2 seconds | Keeps scare spikes of 3 to 10 seconds visible |
| Hold after signal loss, then fade to idle | 10 s on the local route; longer on Streamer.bot integrations | A steady heart rate sends no events on the integration route |
| Pulse-rate cap | Half speed above about 150 bpm | Stays under three flashes per second |
| Test pulse | Off | A fake pulse for setup without a device |

## 6. Privacy and failure: hide the tempo as well as the number, and don't mistake calm for silence

### Privacy turns on what the animation reveals, not just the number

**The owner's two rulings frame this section.** On showing the number: "I think that would be the decision of the streamer". On storage: "that kind of data should never be stored remotely" (project owner, 14 November 2026).

The legal backdrop points the same way. GDPR Article 9 bars processing health data except under listed conditions, which include explicit consent and data the person has clearly made public themselves ([GDPR Art. 9](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/)). Whether a streamer showing their own pulse counts as making it public was not sourced. Washington's My Health My Data Act covers consumer health data, which commentators say includes heart-rate readings, though its effective dates are reported inconsistently and the statute should be checked ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/resources/article/washington-my-health-my-data-act-overview)). The low-risk design processes readings in memory only, stores nothing, sends nothing beyond the PC, and shows nothing until the streamer opts in. That is the researchers' reading, not legal advice.

**Hiding the number does not hide the heart rate if the animation beats at it.** A viewer can count a pulse against a clock as easily as read a figure. So the display choice the owner gives the streamer is only real with a tempo-free mode, where the pulse runs at a fixed tempo and the heart rate drives only size, brightness or colour (inference).

Heart data also leaks through side channels a streamer may not think of. HypeRate Desktop's Discord Rich Presence, for example, shows BPM and heart-rate zone to anyone viewing the profile, updating every 15 seconds ([HypeRate Desktop](https://blog.hyperate.io/post/the-best-free-heart-rate-overlay-for-streamers-and-gamers-hyperate-desktop/)). No reports were found of viewers harassing streamers to spike their heart rate, or inferring health conditions from it. That is an open gap, not evidence that the risk is absent.

### Failure handling must tell a calm streamer from a dead sensor

**On the Streamer.bot integration route, a calm streamer looks exactly like a dead sensor.** The Pulsoid and HypeRate events fire only when the BPM changes, so a steady 72 sends nothing ([v0.2.3 changelog](https://streamer.bot/changelogs/v0.2.3)). A naive timeout would fade a relaxed streamer to idle, so the timeout has to depend on the route.

A local helper can forward every reading. On that route the overlay can hold the last good value for about 10 seconds and then fade to the idle animation Timbre already has (`cfgIdleAnim` in `overlay/core.js`). That matches the one documented precedent: Pulsoid's VRChat prefab hides after 10 seconds without data ([Pulsoid prefab](https://blog.pulsoid.net/post/pulsoid-heart-rate-prefab-for-vrchat-avatars-glowy-heart-rate)). On the integration route, the overlay should rely on HypeRate's Connected and Disconnected events, which Pulsoid lacks ([events index](https://docs.streamer.bot/api/websocket/events)). It also needs a silence window long enough to cover a steady heart rate, and that length has to be measured.

Bad readings and broken links need the same calm handling. No tool documents how it treats zero or impossible values. The researchers suggest an unsourced rule, for the mock server to exercise: reject 0, anything above 220, and jumps of more than about 40 bpm in a second, then hold the last good value.

Broken links on Windows are better documented. A strap usually serves one receiver, so a phone app holding it blocks the PC, unless it is a dual-connection model such as the Polar H10 with firmware 2.1.9 or later in that mode ([Polar support](https://support.polar.com/en/support/can_i_pair_h10_with_several_devices)). Some Windows 10 builds stop delivering readings after a few callbacks; there is a registry workaround, and the Windows 11 status is unknown ([jlennox/HeartRate](https://github.com/jlennox/HeartRate)). Zwift users report Pixel Watches showing zero or dropping off ([Zwift forum](https://forums.zwift.com/t/pixel-watch-4-wont-stay-connected-as-heart-rate-monitor/666747)). And an unreachable device holds up each queued request for about seven seconds ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/uwp/devices-sensors/gatt-client)). The helper should reconnect on its own, as HypeRate Desktop does, and tell the overlay when it loses the sensor so the fade starts at once.

### A heartbeat is a flash source, and red counts separately

**Heart-themed effects lean toward the one colour the flash rule counts separately.** WCAG 2.2's success criterion 2.3.1 treats a pair of opposing luminance changes of 10% or more, with the darker state below 0.80, as a flash. It counts saturated red transitions separately, and allows no more than three of either in any one second. The exception is a flashing area under 0.006 steradians, about 341 × 256 pixels on a 1024 × 768 screen ([WCAG 2.3.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)).

That limit bites sooner than it looks. At 180 bpm there are three beats a second, right at the limit. And because a lub-dub draws two pulses per beat, **a full-strength double pulse reaches three flashes a second at just 90 bpm** (inference). Ofcom's legacy guidance adds that more than five seconds of flashing may be a risk even when compliant; it was read only through a search summary, because the PDF returned 403 ([Ofcom](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/16248/gn_flash.pdf)).

The overlay should therefore halve the visible pulse rate above about 150 bpm and keep full-frame luminance swings under 10%. It should also make the second beat of a lub-dub small, and avoid full-screen pulses of saturated red.

## 7. Testing starts with the mock server and ends with a real strap

**Most of the feature can be tested without a heart.** Timbre already has `mock-sb-server.mjs` standing in for Streamer.bot, plus the `npm run verify` and `npm run verify:render` suites (Timbre `main`). The mock server should gain five scripted heart-rate feeds. The first is a steady value followed by a minute of silence, to imitate the integrations' change-only events. The second is a startle ramp from about 65 to 135 bpm and back over ten seconds. The third is a dropout, and the fourth is invalid values of 0 and 255. The fifth is a sustained 180 bpm, to prove the pulse-rate cap. Render tests can drive the synthesiser from a fixed clock, so that each effect's frame at a given beat phase is reproducible; that approach is this report's recommendation, not something the research tested.

Streamer.bot's own route can be tested without a device too. Streamer.bot documents a HypeRate ID, `internal-testing`, that sends fake heart-rate data ([Streamer.bot HypeRate guide](https://docs.streamer.bot/guide/integrations/hyperate)). It exercises the real integration and the event subscription; it still connects through HypeRate's service, but the data it carries is fake. A flash check at the capped maximum rate belongs in the suite as well, though the PEAT tool that came up in the research may no longer be maintained.

**The last stage cannot be simulated.** It needs a real strap and the owner's own watch, paired with the Windows 11 streaming PC and run through a multi-hour session on both Streamer.bot 1.0.4 and 1.0.7.

## Conclusion: build it, local first

**The recommended source is a Bluetooth chest strap of the Polar H10 class.** It is the most accurate source and the only one that can be private end to end. Second choice is a watch that broadcasts the standard profile, such as a Pixel Watch 2 or later, Fitbit Charge 6, Bluetooth-capable Garmin, Amazfit or Whoop, with the caveat that its own vendor sync continues. Apple Watch and Wear OS Galaxy Watch users come in through Bluetooth rebroadcast apps, and older Tizen Galaxy watches possibly through HeartRateToWeb, once those pass testing.

**The recommended route is a local helper that sends each reading into Streamer.bot with `DoAction`.** A small action rebroadcasts each reading, and the overlays receive it the way they already receive settings. If that relay adds lag or leaves a trail, the fallback is a localhost WebSocket that the overlays read directly.

**Build it: yes.** The first version should carry the local route only, and a synthesised beat filling the four existing values, plus a pulse value tuned into the effects that already pulse. It should show the BPM number off by default, as the streamer's choice, with a tempo-free option. It should also include the flash cap, and stale-signal handling that differs by route. It should leave out Pulsoid, which fails the ruling. HypeRate should stay out too, unless the owner accepts its conditional status after the checks below.

**One decision belongs to the owner before the setup guide is written.** Does the ruling admit a broadcasting watch whose own vendor cloud keeps syncing the same heartbeat, or only a strap worn without its companion app?

**What the research changes is the shape of the feature.** Heartbeat input is less a data integration than a synthesiser with a sensor attached. No cloud route delivers beats at all, and even a strap's RR intervals arrive after the fact. So the pulse on screen is always Timbre's own creation, shaped by a number. Two things follow. The privacy control that matters is over the tempo, not just the digits. And the hard engineering lies in telling a steady heart from a lost signal and in keeping the pulse under the flash limit, not in moving the data.

Several gaps the researchers named are still open, though closing them needs reading or asking rather than a device. **On devices**, four questions are unresolved: whether Huawei, Coros and Suunto watches broadcast; whether Garmin Connect IQ apps can stream heart rate live; whether the Galaxy Watch gained a native broadcast in 2026; and what "Heart Rate on Stream" and "Stream Heart Rate" are, since neither was found. HypeRate's device page also contradicts itself, claiming both 200-plus supported devices and more than 20, and giving both Series 3 and Series 4 as the oldest supported Apple Watch. **On protocols and services**, the Bluetooth SIG's Heart Rate specification text was not fetched, so even the unit for energy expended is disputed between pages. The `@streamerbot/client` type definitions for the heart-rate events were not read. Nobody confirmed whether Pulsoid's OAuth login, including the one inside Streamer.bot, needs the BRO plan, or whether an open-source client ID is allowed. HypeRate's API key terms, rate limits, update rate and definition of "indie" were not found. **On safety and law**, several items were not found or not checked: a Twitch or YouTube creator-facing rule on flashing overlays, the details of the Harding flash test, Ofcom's thresholds in primary text, and Nevada and Connecticut health-data law. Nor did any study measure device lag, or test whether viewers notice a pulse out of step with real beats.

The following checks must be done hands-on before any build.
